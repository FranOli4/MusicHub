# Music, concentration and sleep: what the research says

This note sums up the research on background music during focused work and at bedtime. It also lists what MusicHub's generator does as a result. Evidence ratings: **strong** means replicated and supported by meta-analyses; **moderate** means several studies agree on the direction; **weak** means one study, mixed results, or an inference from nearby findings; **marketing** means it is mostly commercial claims.

Some numbers come from abstracts or secondary summaries, not the full papers. They are marked *(secondhand)*.

## 1. What disrupts concentration: changing sound

The best-replicated finding is the **irrelevant sound effect**. Sound you are trying to ignore still disrupts tasks that keep things in order, like reading, verbal recall or following an argument. What drives the disruption is the *number of distinct acoustic changes*, not the loudness or the meaning of the sound.

- A sequence of different tones disrupts recall more than one tone repeated (Jones & Macken, 1993).
- Continuous pitch glides do **not** disrupt recall. The same glides broken up by short silences do (Jones, Macken & Murray, 1993). Segmentation matters more than pitch movement.
- Staccato instrumental music disrupted serial recall more than legato music did (Schlittmeier, Hellbrück et al., 2008). Later work by the same group could predict the disruption from psychoacoustic "fluctuation strength".
- Vocal music disrupts more than instrumental music (Salamé & Baddeley, 1989).

**Evidence: strong** for the effect itself; **moderate** for the legato/staccato difference.
**What it means for us:** few clearly separate onsets, legato phrasing, soft attacks, a continuous sustained bed, no stop-start silences, and changes that arrive gradually.

## 2. Words are the worst distraction

- A Bayesian meta-analysis of 65 studies found that noise, speech and music all slightly harm reading. Intelligible speech and lyrics harm it most (Vasilev, Kirkby & Angele, 2018). Instrumental music on its own came out at about zero *(secondhand)*.
- Reading comprehension was lowest with lyrical music, whether people liked the music or not (Perham & Currie, 2014).

**Evidence: strong.**
**What it means for us:** the app is purely instrumental. A familiar *hymn tune* can still bring its words to mind. That is untested (**weak**), but it is cheap to avoid, so Think quotes famous hymn tunes less often.

## 3. The "Mozart effect" is really about arousal and mood

- The original study reported a short spatial-reasoning boost after 10 minutes of Mozart (Rauscher, Shaw & Ky, 1993). The boost lasted about 10–15 minutes.
- A meta-analysis of about 40 studies and over 3,000 people found that *any* music beat silence about as much as Mozart did (d ≈ 0.37–0.38). Mozart against other music was only d ≈ 0.15, with signs of publication bias (Pietschnig, Voracek & Formann, 2010).
- Fast, major-key Mozart helped and a slow, minor-key Albinoni adagio did not. Once arousal and mood were controlled for, the advantage disappeared (Thompson, Schellenberg & Husain, 2001). Tempo mainly moves arousal and mode mainly moves mood (Husain et al., 2002).

**Evidence: strong** that there is nothing special about Mozart; **moderate** that pleasant, moderately arousing music helps through mood.
**What it means for us:** keep the styles you enjoy. Liking the music is part of the effect. Lean towards major keys in Think, and let the user set how energetic it is.

## 4. Tempo, loudness and the type of task

- Instrumental music that was both **fast and loud** lowered reading comprehension below silence. Fast-but-soft music did not (Thompson, Schellenberg & Letnic, 2012).
- Overall, background music roughly cancels out on cognition: it hurts reading, slightly hurts memory, and improves mood (Kämpfe, Sedlmeier & Renkewitz, 2011).
- Music generally impaired complex tasks. Livelier, more complex music could *help simple tasks* for people who are not prone to boredom (Gonzalez & Aiello, 2019).
- Self-chosen music reduced mind-wandering during a dull vigilance task, by raising arousal (Kiss & Linnell, 2021 and 2024). People choose calm instrumental music for reading, and livelier music for monotonous work (Kiss & Linnell, 2023).
- Extraverts tolerate busier background music better than introverts do (Furnham & Bradley, 1997). Replications are only partial.

**Evidence: moderate.**
**What it means for us:** moderate Think tempos, controlled loudness, and a **Focus level** setting. *Deep work* is calmer and slower; *Routine tasks* is livelier.

## 5. Complexity and habituation

Liking tends to follow an inverted U: music that is too simple bores, and music that is too complex demands attention. A review of 57 studies supports this, though how many studies really fit the pattern is disputed (Chmiel & Schubert, 2017). No study has measured how quickly background music should change to avoid habituation without drawing attention. Combined with the changing-state findings, the safe inference is that **slow changes at section level are cheap and frequent changes from note to note are costly**.

**Evidence: weak.**
**What it means for us:** melodies move mostly by step and are fairly predictable, with leaps resolving back. New material arrives every minute or two. Larger "chapter" changes, such as a key change to a related key, happen every few minutes, smoothly.

Music supplied by Brain.fm; no independent replication yet. | Weak to preliminary |
| Binaural beats | One meta-analysis found g ≈ 0.45, mostly when listened to *before* the task (Garcia-Argibay et al., 2019). Later reviews and studies are contradictory (Robison et al., 2022; Ingendoh et al., 2023). | Weak or mixed; marketing-heavy |
| White or pink noise | Small benefits for children with ADHD (Söderlund et al., 2007 onwards); sometimes harmful for others. Nothing specific to brown noise. | Weak to moderate, ADHD only |

**What it means for us:** an optional **Focus pulse** (16 Hz, shallow, Think only) and an optional **Soft noise bed**. Both are off by default and described honestly in Settings. No binaural beats.

## 7. Sleep

- A Cochrane review of 13 randomised trials (n = 1,007) found that music improved self-rated sleep quality by about one standard deviation (PSQI −2.79), with moderate certainty. Evidence for falling asleep sooner and sleeping longer is thinner, about 3 trials each (Jespersen et al., 2022).
- Studies typically used instrumental music at 60–80 BPM: soft, smooth, simple, with no accents or percussion, for 30–45 minutes.
- Arousal follows tempo. A short silent pause after music lowered heart rate and breathing *below* baseline (Bernardi, Porta & Sleight, 2006), which supports ending with a slow fade to silence.
- The music people actually choose for sleep is legato, mid-register and low in rhythmic activity, but not always slow (Dickson & Schubert, 2020). A study of 225,000 Spotify sleep tracks found them softer, slower and more acoustic (Scarratt et al., 2023).
- Continuous noise as a sleep aid: very low-quality evidence (Riedy et al., 2021).

**Evidence: moderate.**
**What it means for us:** slow tempos, soft attacks, a dark tone, very small dynamic swings, and a **wind-down** over the first 30 minutes (gradually slower, sparser, softer and darker). The sleep timer fades out over 5 minutes.

## 8. Tone colour

The psychoacoustic model of annoyance rises with loudness, sharpness (weight in the high frequencies), roughness and fluctuation (Zwicker & Fastl). Sudden loud onsets cause startle. Reverb smooths onsets, though that is an inference rather than a tested finding.

**What it means for us:** a gentle top-end roll-off in Think, a much darker tone in Sleep, a slow master leveller and limiter so loudness stays even, and no high, piercing lead lines.
